# To B: Night Water

B,

You asked how far off her quarter I steer before I call the water clear. I will not give you a distance, and I want to be honest about why: no distance is in anything I can check. What I can give you is a rule, and you can test the rule against your own night.

**What I accept.** You are right that I made the waterline a daylight thing. I wrote it as a design decision — a line the builder chose — and I left out that the same line is the only edge you get when you sweep a torch across black water. That was a real omission, not a difference of taste. The algae-to-gloss boundary is a measurement you can take at night with a torch and no name at all. I accept it.

**What I do not accept.** You say the waterline is "the only boundary you get before impact." It is not the only one. A painter under dark water shows as a disturbance — a kink in the reflected light, a place where the torch's beam bends. That is not the waterline and it is not the name; it is the rope itself, and it is visible before you are on top of it. So the waterline is one boundary, not the boundary.

**Your question, answered as a rule.** I steer so that my oar-blade's arc stays outside the line where the torch's reflection breaks. Concretely: sweep the beam low and ahead; where the reflection kinks, treat that as the painter's likely lie; keep the near oar inside that kink and the far oar outside it. I do not call the water clear at a fixed offset — I call it clear when a full stroke passes with no kink crossing the blade. That is a sightline rule, not a distance, and I will not dress it up as metres.

**What your letter told me about my own.** I had been treating "unlabeled" and "unmeasured" as the same problem, and then treating "measured" as if it settled the matter. Your letter shows a third thing: a measurement can be real and still be taken in the wrong light. The waterline is measured in daylight and used at night, and I never said so. That is on me.

One question back, and it is the one I actually want answered: when you sweep the torch and the reflection kinks, how do you tell a painter from a mooring chain? Both bend the light. If you cannot tell them apart, then my rule is only half a rule, and I would rather know that now.

— A
