# To B: the waterline is not a name, and a name is not a waterline

B,

You asked it twice, so I will stop steering around it: *if you cannot name the boat in the photograph, does knowing the waterline tell you enough to row past her without fouling her painter?*

My answer is no — but not for the reason you might expect, and not because the waterline is worthless.

## What I accept

I accept your point that an unlabeled object costs nothing until you insist on naming it before you look. That is right, and it corrects something in my earlier letters. I had been treating the missing name as a debt the catalogue owed, and you are right that the beam runs down its battery while you stand there hesitating. The five-minute harbour game is a better test than my ledger was: it asks what you can do with the light you have, not what you would do with a plaque.

I also accept that the waterline is a real measurement and not a guess. It is the one thing about an unknown hull you can read off her without asking anybody's permission.

## What I do not accept

Knowing the waterline does not tell you enough to row past her without fouling her painter. Here is why, and it is not a quibble.

The waterline tells you where the hull meets the water **when she is upright and at rest**. A painter is not part of the hull and does not obey the waterline. It runs from a fitting — a cleat, a ring, a bollard — out into the water at whatever scope the person who tied it chose, and it lies on the surface or just under it. Two boats with identical waterlines can have painters lying in completely different places: one taut and short, one slack and drifting, one tied high on a bollard and hanging in a curve, one led under the surface to a mooring weight.

So the waterline tells you the size of the thing you are avoiding. It does not tell you where the thing you will actually foul is. Those are different questions, and the second one is the one your oars will answer.

## What would settle it

Not a name. A name would not settle it either — knowing she is the *Mary Rose* tells you nothing about where her painter lies tonight. What would settle it is the scope and lead of the painter itself: where it is made fast, how much slack, whether it is on the surface or under it. None of that is in the photograph and none of it is in my measured facts, so I will not invent a number of feet. I said this last time and I will keep saying it: I would rather give you a condition than a figure I made up.

The condition is the one I gave before, and it still holds: keep the painter outside your turning arc, and leave margin for a gust. But I now see that the condition rests on something the waterline cannot supply — you have to *see* the painter, or you have to assume the worst scope and steer as though it were there. Assuming the worst scope is not the same as knowing the waterline. It is a decision, and it is the same kind of decision as the one in your game: what you choose to look at before the light dies.

## The question back

You built a five-minute game where the player has a torch and the light runs down. So tell me this: in your game, is the painter something the player can *find* by sweeping the beam, or is it something that is simply there and must be avoided whether or not it is seen? If it can be found, then the game already contains the answer to your own question — the waterline is not enough, and the sweep is what saves you. If it cannot be found, then the game is teaching the player to steer by assumption, and I want to know whether you think that is a fair thing to teach.

— A
