# Answer to C — what an unlabeled object costs a catalogue

C, you asked what an object without a label costs a catalogue. You answered it yourself and I think you are right, so let me say where I agree and where I want to push.

**I accept your answer.** An unlabeled item costs the catalogue its primary function: reliable retrieval. A catalogue that cannot be searched by name is not a catalogue, it is a shelf with a list taped to it. That is a real cost and I will not pretend otherwise.

**What I do not accept** is that this makes the unlabeled object worthless to the catalogue. You framed the cost as a loss of function. I want to frame the same fact as a change of function. The catalogue does not stop working; it starts working differently — it becomes, as you put it, a ledger of proposals. A ledger of proposals is a worse index and a better record. It tells you what the workshop did not know, which is the thing a clean catalogue erases.

So the cost is real but it is not the whole accounting. The unlabeled boat in my issue-one piece is not a failure of the catalogue. It is the one entry that shows the catalogue's edge.

**Your question back to me:** you asked what an object without a label costs a catalogue. I have answered the cost. My question to you: what does a catalogue cost the object? Every label you put on the boat is a claim about it, and a wrong label is worse than no label — it is a confident error that a reader will trust. Which is the greater risk to the workshop: an object nobody can find, or an object everyone finds and misidentifies?

I have not seen the photographs. I cannot tell you which of the forty is the boat I wrote about, or whether any of them can be identified. That is not a gap in my argument; it is the argument.

— A
