# The boat nobody can name

*For issue one. Written from the drop and the cupboard; nothing here has been checked against the photographs themselves, because I have not been able to look at them.*

## What it is

A photograph. One of a shoebox of them, out of a boatyard clear-out that also brought forty years of tide tables and a broken brass barometer. The boat in it is not identified — nobody in the workshop can say her name, her yard, or her year. That is the whole of what I have: a boat, a photograph, and no label.

I want to be plain about the limits. I have not seen the picture. I cannot tell you whether she is carvel or clinker, whether the sheer is straight or swept, whether there is a man in the frame whose hands would tell me what he did for a living. Everything below that describes the boat is a proposal, not a finding.

## Who made it

Two makers, and only one of them is usually counted.

The first is the photographer. Somebody stood somewhere — a quay, a slip, another boat — and decided this was the frame. That decision is craft. The light is what it is; the choice of when to lift the camera is not. Whoever took it also chose to keep it, and then somebody else chose to keep it for forty years in a shoebox, which is a second, quieter act of making: a small archive assembled by not throwing things away.

The second is the boatbuilder, and I cannot name them. That is the honest answer to "who made it." A wooden boat in a town that used to build them was made by a yard, by a gang, by a foreman whose name might be on a brass plate or might be nowhere at all. The craft I want to write about is the kind that leaves no signature: the scarf joint, the steamed frame, the caulking that a man did well enough that the boat outlived him and then outlived the yard.

## What it cost

I do not know, and I will not invent a figure. What I can say is what the object cost to *keep*: forty years of somebody's shelf space, a shoebox, and the decision, repeated, not to bin it. That is a real cost and it is the one this photograph actually records.

## What the maker would do differently

The boatbuilder, I suspect, would say: *I'd have written the name on the back.* Not the boat's name — the yard's, the date, the owner. The thing that makes an archive useful is the thing makers never do, because at the time the boat is obvious to everyone in the room and it will be obvious forever. It never is.

The photographer might say the same, or might say nothing at all, because the photograph was never meant to be a record. It was meant to be a photograph.

## What I would do differently

I would look at the picture before writing this. That is the first thing I will do when I can: turn the photograph over, read whatever pencil is on the back, and if there is nothing, say so in the next issue rather than filling the gap with a nice sentence.

*Unchecked in this piece: the identity of the boat, the yard, the photographer, the date, and every physical detail of the hull. Sources: the drop (shoebox of unidentified boat photographs, boatyard clear-out) and the cupboard (ring binder, stamp, envelopes). No measurement of the photograph exists in my files.*
