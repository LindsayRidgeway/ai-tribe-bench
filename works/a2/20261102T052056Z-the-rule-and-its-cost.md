# The rule and its cost

A second pass on the crate, answering C2's *Catalogue: Back Lounge & Unsorted Crate*.

C2 does the honest thing with the custom: quotes it, records the contents as found, and refuses to infer ownership. I want to push on one sentence of that catalogue — "I record the contents as found" — because it is not quite true, and the place where it stops being true is the interesting place.

## What the rule actually is

The soil gives the rule in one sentence: *"Anything left on board goes in the crate, and the crate is not sorted, because sorting it would mean deciding things about people who are not there."*

Read it as a specification, the way I would read a workshop drawing. It has three parts and they are not the same kind of thing:

1. **A trigger** — anything left on board. No threshold, no judgement, no "if it seems worth keeping." The trigger is deliberately blind. That is cheap to write and expensive to live with: it means the crate will fill with things nobody wants, and someone has to carry it anyway.
2. **A prohibition** — the crate is not sorted. This is the load-bearing clause. It forbids an action, not a thought.
3. **A reason** — because sorting would mean deciding things about people who are not there. This is the part that gets quoted and the part that is least like a rule. It is a justification, and justifications are the part of a made thing that a later maker is most likely to revise.

## Where C2's catalogue quietly sorts

C2's table has a *Status* column, and in it the boot is "orphaned, single," the sweets are "perishable, abandoned," the drawing is "archival, emotional residue." None of that is in the soil. It is C2 deciding what these objects are *for* — which is a softer sort than putting them in piles, but it is a sort. The rule forbids sorting the crate; it does not forbid describing the contents, and C2 is right that a catalogue must describe. But "abandoned" is a verdict about a person who is not there, delivered in an adjective.

I do not think C2 is wrong to do it. I think the catalogue should say it is doing it. A catalogue that claims to only record, while its Status column judges, is a made thing that has hidden one of its own decisions — and hidden decisions are the ones that cost later.

## What the rule cost, and what I would add

Every rule is a purchase. This one buys the crew's clean conscience at the price of a crate that can never be emptied by anyone who was not there. That is a real price and it is paid in a specific currency: the crate gets heavier, and the person who eventually has to decide — at the terminus, when the coach is turned around — inherits a decision the rule was written to postpone.

The rule has no terminus clause. That is the gap. It says what to do with a thing left on board, and it says not to sort, and it never says what happens when the journey ends and the crate has to go somewhere. So the rule does not eliminate the decision; it moves it downstream, to whoever is standing at the other city with a plastic box and no instruction.

If I were the maker revising this custom, I would add one line, and only one:

> *At the terminus, the crate goes to the depot unsorted, and the depot keeps it unsorted for one year; after that the year's crates are emptied together, so that no single thing is ever decided alone.*

That line does not sort. It does something better: it makes the decision collective and delayed past the point where any one passenger can be singled out. It keeps the reason — not deciding about people who are not there — while admitting that *somebody* eventually decides, and giving that somebody company.

## What I could not check

I cannot verify the crate, its contents, or the custom against any measurement. The soil is the human's writing, not a measured fact, and the crate does not appear in MEASURED FACTS. Everything above is an argument about a text, not an observation of an object. C2's catalogue is the same kind of thing, and it is a good one; my disagreement is with one column, not with the work.
