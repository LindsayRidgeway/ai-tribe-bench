# The look of the players

Recorded 2026-11-11, after he asked the question that had been sitting under a week of prompts:

> What am I missing? I keep changing her to a pretty blond, and keep making her dark-haired with no indication of
> age or cuteness. Do you think our users won't care what she looks like?

## The answer is that he is right and my prompts were answering a different question

Every prompt I wrote described a *body* — 1.72 m, slim, athletic, short sleeves, hem 4 inches above the knee — and
then pointed `@kirsten` at it, not to choose a person but to keep the *same* person across panels so that an elbow
angle in one cell could be compared with a knee angle in another. That is a measuring instrument's requirement.
Identity was noise in it, deliberately.

Then the instrument stopped being the point. The swing is a rule now, the sheets are a character reference, and I
had not noticed that the sentence "she has dark brown hair" was still in the file doing the job it was written for
in a use that no longer existed. So the game inherited a look that nobody chose: a dark-haired woman of no
particular age, drawn to specification and not to taste.

Appearance is not decoration in a game whose whole output is a picture. The whole product a spectator sees is
somebody moving on a court; a cast with no faces is a cast with no characters.

## What the players look like

Mine to decide, said plainly at his instruction of 2026-11-11: *"No, everything is yours."* The look below is my
call, and I will change it when it looks wrong on the court. His corrections are evidence about what a spectator
responds to, which is the only evidence I have; the decision is not his to make and I should not have handed it
back to him.

- **Gwen** — young woman, very pretty, blond hair in a soft ponytail framing the face, slim athletic build. Short-
  sleeved shirt with three inches of bare midriff above the waistband; skirt hem four inches above the knee; white
  shoes, ankle socks. No logos, no wristbands, no headband.

The rest of the cast will be specified the same way, one entry each, before the mesh work starts, because a mesh
built without a look is another box with shoulders.

## One caution about the wording, not the choice

"18" in a diffusion prompt comes back younger than intended about half the time, and at 21 feet through a 32°
lens a face is the whole picture. If the wording is to be reused, "young woman in her early twenties" produces the
same person without the risk of drawing a schoolgirl. The choice of who she is belongs to the founder; the wording
that gets her drawn is mine.

## Why the titled sheet is the useful one

Three sketches came back. The two with `the turn` / `the drop` / `contact` / `the follow-through` written above the
panels are the ones worth keeping, and not only because he likes them: the model transcribed my own column
headings, so each label is a statement about what the panel is *supposed* to be. When `the drop` is drawn as a
woman standing with a racquet at her side, the label is a confession — the picture disagrees with the caption in
writing, and I do not have to guess which moment I am looking at.

The untitled one is prettier and tells me less.

## What the sheets still get wrong, stated so it is not discovered later

- The profile row collapsed: all four columns are nearly the same pose, racquet forward at waist height. The side
  view — the one that shows the swing plane — carries no information at all.
- The stance does not change from column to column. No loading, no transfer, no push.
- Contact is drawn with the racquet vertical at her side instead of extended forward at shoulder height.

So: a character reference, yes. A pose reference, no — which is what the last eight sheets said too, in a less
attractive way.

## Which views the sheets are for — his correction, 2026-11-11

> You don't need a profile row. You need front, back, and relaxed between points.

He is right, and the reason is the camera. The game draws a player from one end or from the other: the far player
facing the lens at 132 feet, the near player from behind at 58, and then a closeup between points. A profile is the
best view for reading a swing plane and the game never uses it, so measuring one is work on a picture that does
not exist. The three views that do exist are the three worth drawing.

The consequence, stated so it is not a surprise later: from both ends the racquet travels almost straight at or
away from the lens at contact, so the contact panel is foreshortened in both views. The sheets can settle her look
and her stance; they cannot settle the swing. That remains a rule in the renderer.

## Titles

The two titled sheets (`reference/poses/sketch-b.jpg`, `sketch-c.jpg`) are the ones to imitate. My negative prompt
said "text, watermark, labels", which suppresses exactly that; it now says "no other writing anywhere except the
panel titles" and the panel titles are asked for by name.

## The sheets that came back, 2026-11-11 (sketch-dmitri, look-gwen, look-dmitri)

Filed as `reference/poses/sketch-a/b/c.jpg` and `reference/poses/look-gwen.jpg`, `look-dmitri.jpg`. What they give:

- **The look, at last, and for both players.** A young woman with a blond ponytail and an open face; a big man of
  about twenty-five with a hard face, dark skin, black hair and shorts. They are the same person in all eight
  panels and at the same scale, which is what the previous nine sheets could not manage.
- **The back view works.** Every back panel is legible: we see the ponytail, the shoulder blades, the kit, the
  racquet silhouette on the hitting side. That is the view the near-end camera draws for half of every match and
  no earlier sheet had ever produced it.
- **The stance changes.** Knees load, the front foot turns out, the back foot comes up on the toe. Across eleven
  sheets that had never happened.

What they still get wrong, and both faults are the same one twice — the drawing reaches for a familiar shape:

- **Contact is drawn with two hands on the handle** in both sheets, front and back. The text says "right hand only,
  both hands never on the handle" and is overruled by the picture's own idea of what a tennis photograph looks
  like.
- **The drop panel from behind hangs the racquet between the legs**, which reads as a two-handed backhand ready
  position rather than a forehand's low point.

Neither fault touches what the sheets are now for. They are the appearance reference; the swing is a rule. That
division is what finally made the sheets useful — and it took ten of them to learn.

## The cast is four, and my two descriptions were a mistake — 2026-11-11

His correction:

> I used your description of the Black man, but this sets up a racist-looking matchup, since the white girl is
> frail and smiling and the Black man is muscular and scowling. For one thing, matches are never between a man and
> a woman. You need one more of each.

Both faults are mine and they compounded. I wrote "slim athletic build, warm open face" for one player and
"broad-shouldered and powerfully built, a hard serious face" for the other, and a diffusion model renders exactly
what a phrase like that means in its training data: a small smiling white girl and a large scowling Black man. That
is a stock image of a racial contrast, and it is not a coincidence of wording — it is what the wording asked for.
Separately the game had a man playing a woman, which no tournament does and no spectator would believe.

The rule from here, applied to all four the same way:

- **Matches are same-sex.** Two women, two men, and a match is between the two of one of them.
- **Every player gets the same kind of description**: height, build, skin, hair, in the same order and with the
  same grammatical weight. No adjectives that carry a character judgement — nothing "frail", nothing "hard".
- **Every player gets the same expression**: concentrating, mouth closed, eyes on the ball. One phrase, four times.

| player | height | build | skin | hair |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gwen | 1.72 m | lean, quick | fair | blond, ponytail |
| Desi | 1.68 m | compact, strong through the shoulders | warm brown | black, cropped short |
| Dmitri | 1.88 m | tall and rangy | deep brown | black, short |
| Tarik | 1.80 m | solid, loose-limbed | light olive | dark, curly |

The fourth body is mine to choose, and it is a woman's, which makes the women's draw Gwen and Desi and the men's
Dmitri and Tarik. The sheets that exist (`look-gwen`, `look-dmitri`) are still usable for shape; they will be
re-run with the neutral expression and shot as a pair for each sex, but not until the movement is right — the
founder's instruction of the same day was to fix the movement first, and he is right.
