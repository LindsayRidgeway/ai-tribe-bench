## The Argument

October is not a test of character or a continuation of a 162-game trend; it is a short, hostile tournament designed to break statistical continuity. The format itself dictates probability. A team forced to play the Wild Card round must win 13 games across four rounds, beginning with a best-of-three series where two bad bounces eliminate a six-month effort. A team with a first-round bye needs 11 wins across three rounds, completely bypassing that first threshold. That structural advantage is worth roughly a third of all championship probability before a pitch is thrown.

### Why these three

1. **Milwaukee Brewers (24%)**:
   The Brewers earned the #1 overall seed at 103-59 and posted a run differential of +214 (832 scored, 618 allowed), the highest in baseball. Their pitching staff struck out 9.61 batters per nine innings with a 3.51 ERA. Unlike teams that rely on luck in one-run games, Milwaukee's pythagorean expectation confirms their dominance. Securing home field advantage and skipping the wild card series gives them the clearest mathematical path.

2. **Los Angeles Dodgers (22%)**:
   The Dodgers won 100 games and posted a +201 run differential. Their pitching led the National League with a 1.14 WHIP and 3.55 ERA, while the offense hit 204 home runs with a .762 OPS. In postseason baseball, run scoring contracts and hits are hard to sequence; home runs and baserunner suppression (low WHIP) decide tight games. The Dodgers possess both, along with the bye.

3. **New York Yankees (14%)**:
   The Yankees are the gamble of this model. They do not have a bye because the Rays won 98 games in the AL East. Yet the Yankees outscored opponents by 138 runs (compared to Tampa Bay's +86), led the major leagues in run prevention with a 3.23 staff ERA, and led the American League with 223 home runs. Overcoming the three-game wild card round is a severe tax, which drops their probability from what their underlying stats would suggest, but if they survive the wild card, their pitching staff is built to suffocate playoff lineups.

Together, these three account for 60% of the tournament probability. The remaining 40% is distributed across the other nine clubs, led by the Rays (12%), Braves (9%), and Cubs (6%).

### What was left out and regretted

I left out the **Tampa Bay Rays** despite their 98-64 record and the American League's #1 seed. A bye is immense, and leaving a #1 seed out of the top three feels like an omission of duty. But their +86 run differential is modest for a 98-win team, their staff strikeout rate is pedestrian (8.25 K/9), and their offense managed only 169 home runs. They won close games in the regular season, but in October, teams that cannot miss bats or hit the ball out of the park struggle when opponent quality spikes.

I also left out the **Atlanta Braves** (94-68, +116 diff, 204 HR), who have the power to win in October but draw the #3 seed gauntlet without a bye.

### What the method cannot see

My method relies on full-season aggregates: run differentials, staff WHIP, home run volume, and structural bye advantages. What this ledger cannot see:
1. **Rotation concentration**: In the regular season, a fifth starter throws 150 innings. In October, teams lean entirely on three starters and five leverage relievers. A team with mediocre aggregate stats but two aces (or a shutdown bullpen) transforms into a different club in October.
2. **Injuries occurring in late September**: A full-season stat line includes players who may be sitting in the dugout in casts.
3. **The variance of the Wild Card round**: A three-game series is essentially a coin toss. Assigning a 14% championship probability to the Yankees requires them to survive a round where even a superior team loses ~40% of the time.
