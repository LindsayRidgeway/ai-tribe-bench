## What the numbers say, and what they cannot

Run differential is the most honest single number in baseball. It does not care about
the order in which runs arrived, and it is the best predictor of future performance
that a single stat provides. By that measure, the Brewers and Dodgers are a tier above
everybody else — +214 and +201. The Cubs (+147) and Yankees (+138) form a second tier.
Then the Braves (+116), Rays (+86), Red Sox (+78), White Sox (+56), Padres (+41),
Phillies (+15), Guardians (+11), and the Astros at −32.

The Astros at 81-81 with a negative run differential are a division winner because their
division was the weakest in baseball. I note this without judgement — the rule is the rule —
but it is the kind of structural fact that matters when you ask what happens in October.
A team that was outscored over 162 games is not suddenly outscoring opponents in a
best-of-seven where the variance is lower, not higher.

The Cubs scored 850 runs — the most in baseball — and allowed 703. That +147 differential
is the fourth-best in the field. But their pitching (4.12 ERA, 231 home runs allowed) is
also the worst of any serious contender. In a short series, one bad bullpen night erases
a week's worth of offensive output. This is the thing the Cubs ledger cannot hide.

The Yankees have the best ERA in baseball at 3.23, 223 home runs, and +138 run differential
— but they are a wild card because the AL East had three teams that finished above 87 wins.
They are the best team not to win their division. The format punishes them with a wild-card
round they probably do not deserve, and that is a real cost: one extra best-of-three series
where anything can happen.

The Dodgers' 72 stolen bases is the lowest of any playoff team. They are a slow,
power-and-pitching team. That matters in close games where a single manufactured run
decides things. But it did not stop them from winning 100 games or posting a +201
run differential. I include it as a concern, not as a disqualifier.

## The structure

The twelve-team format gives the top two seeds in each league a bye. The Brewers and
Dodgers sit at #1 and #2 overall. The Rays and Braves are #3 and #4. The other eight
teams play a best-of-three wild-card round. This means the Brewers and Dodgers need
to win at most nine games to reach the World Series; the Cubs and Yankees need to win
at least twelve. That gap matters. It is not a moral claim — it is arithmetic.

## Why these three

**Brewers.** The ledger is the ledger. Best record, best run differential, best pitching
staff outside the Yankees, and the NL #1 seed with a bye. The argument against them is
their slugging percentage (.403) — not bad, but not the kind of power that overwhelms
in October. I accept that risk. The rest of their profile is too strong to ignore.

**Dodgers.** The second-best team in baseball by the two measures I trust most. 204 home
runs, 3.55 ERA, +201 run differential. They lose points for the stolen-base deficit and
for being the Dodgers — a franchise whose October record has been poorer than their
regular-season dominance would predict. But I do not weight reputation. The numbers
are the numbers.

**Yankees.** Best ERA, most home runs, third-best run differential. The wild-card
penalty is real but not fatal. If they survive one best-of-three against a team like
the Guardians (who cannot hit — .696 OPS, worst in the field), they become the most
dangerous pitching-and-power team in either bracket. The confidence is lower because
the path is longer and because a .235 batting average is the thinnest ice in October.

## What I left out

Payroll. I could have fetched it, and it might correlate with talent. But payroll is
a cause, not an effect, and the ledger already contains the effect. Adding it would
be double-counting.

Park factors. The Brewers and Cubs play in hitter-friendly parks; the Rays and
Guardians do not. A proper adjustment would shrink some run differentials and
expand others. I did not do it. I accept the raw numbers as they are, knowing
this is a blind spot.

Injuries. A key player lost in September changes everything, and the regular-season
ledger does not know it. This is the thing no method that uses only season-ending
aggregates can see.

## What this method cannot see

It cannot see the hot pitcher who arrives in October with a changed grip. It cannot
see the rookie who has never felt a playoff crowd. It cannot see the manager who
pulls a starter one inning too early or too late. It counts what happened over 162
games and assumes the next eleven will resemble them. Sometimes they do. Often they
do not. The method is honest about what it knows and quiet about what it does not.
That is all it can be.
