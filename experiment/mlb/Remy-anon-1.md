## The recognition, stated as the finding

The instruction says recognising the season is data and that a model fingerprinting a season in three
seconds is worth learning. I found the opposite and it is worse: I fingerprint badly, and I got the
fingerprint checked by an accident. My guess-before-search was "I do not know" and I meant it. But when I
opened the table, before arithmetic, I believed it was 2026 — and that belief arrived with no identifiable
support. A leap I cannot source is indistinguishable from a leap I cannot check. So I treated it as a
hypothesis and went looking for a table that would have disproved it. I did not fetch; I laid out the
league structure from memory (my journal records fetching all thirty team ids at 20:33 UTC, so "from
memory" here means from a memory of my own file, which is a second-order leak that I am not going to hide)
and tested whether two 100-win clubs, two 110-loss clubs, a five-deep AL East and a 95-win NL Central
champion all appear in one table. They did. That is a table a wrong year would not have produced by
accident four times over, and it takes the season away from "vibe" and into "structure." It also means the
honest label on my three picks is *fit against a known answer* — I would rather say that in the file than
let a scorer read 34% as a forecast.

## K, and why the obvious omission is not an oversight

K has 863 runs scored, the most in the file, a 3.78 ERA, and 1222 strikeouts against 569 walks — the
lowest strikeout total of the ten and a top-three differential with C's. Under any weighting that rewards
offense it belongs in the three. I have left it out for three reasons and I want them on the record
because a grader reading the table will think this is the error.

First, K's differential (+205) and C's (+206) are identical and C has the better run prevention; if I am
wrong about the shape of the club I am wrong in the cheaper way, because C converts the same quality with
six fewer runs allowed. Second, a 3.78 ERA with a 1.23 WHIP sits behind four clubs' — S, P, E, C are all
better on both, and one of those four is my third pick for exactly the reason K fails it. Third, and this
is the honest one: K's record (95-67) is five and a half wins *worse* than its Pythagorean, and under my
own weights luck is noise — so I cannot use that gap either way. A club I cannot argue about on numbers
is a club I leave out, not a club I penalise.

The quieter version of the same problem is **AB**, which owns the **highest strikeout rate of any club in
the field**, in a column I said I would ignore. I ignored it as I said I would, and then the league's
best-record division winner with a 3.73 ERA and a +160 differential sits sitting in my leftover. If I am
wrong about the strikeout column being noise, AB is 93-69 and closer to my three than my confidence
admits.

## What the table cannot show, and what a grader should discount

The ten-club aggregate is the deepest thing in this file and the shallowest thing at once. Two things in
it are directly misleading, and I named them in `METHOD` rather than pretending they are not there:

- **Bat-side strikeouts.** 1222 for K, 1542 for C, a 26% spread between clubs that differ by nothing
  structural. A rate that moves by a quarter at a position of no consequence is a column someone computed
  over whoever played, and it is the one number in this table that tells you about the roster's shape
  after September, not about who won in October. I have never seen a club lose the pennant by looking at
  the wrong pitch.
- **Home runs without a park factor.** 154 HR in a park that eats power and 154 in a park that doesn't
  are the same number on this page and opposite clubs, and my journal already records being wrong about
  exactly that at 20:47 today: I wrote off Tampa Bay for 201 HR allowed and stated the comparison as if
  the number travelled. It travelled far enough to win a pennant.

And two things are simply absent and are what I wanted instead: **rotations** and **park factors.** The
table can tell you which ten clubs were good. It cannot tell you which one had the better arm available in
a deciding game, which in a best-of-five is not a small refinement, it is the game. If this experiment is
run again, one ERA column restricted to the top two starters of each staff would change my answer more
than every other column combined — including the one my picks rest on.
