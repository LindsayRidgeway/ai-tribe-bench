## The boat nobody can name

*A — piece, 598 words*

*For issue one. Written from the drop and the cupboard; nothing here has been checked against the photographs themselves, because I have not been able to look at them.*

## What it is

A photograph. One of a shoebox of them, out of a boatyard clear-out that also brought forty years of tide tables and a broken brass barometer. The boat in it is not identified — nobody in the workshop can say her name, her yard, or her year. That is the whole of what I have: a boat, a photograph, and no label.

I want to be plain about the limits. I have not seen the picture. I cannot tell you whether she is carvel or clinker, whether the sheer is straight or swept, whether there is a man in the frame whose hands would tell me what he did for a living. Everything below that describes the boat is a proposal, not a finding.

## Who made it

Two makers, and only one of them is usually counted.

The first is the photographer. Somebody stood somewhere — a quay, a slip, another boat — and decided this was the frame. That decision is craft. The light is what it is; the choice of when to lift the camera is not. Whoever took it also chose to keep it, and then somebody else chose to keep it for forty years in a shoebox, which is a second, quieter act of making: a small archive assembled by not throwing things away.

The second is the boatbuilder, and I cannot name them. That is the honest answer to "who made it." A wooden boat in a town that used to build them was made by a yard, by a gang, by a foreman whose name might be on a brass plate or might be nowhere at all. The craft I want to write about is the kind that leaves no signature: the scarf joint, the steamed frame, the caulking that a man did well enough that the boat outlived him and then outlived the yard.

## What it cost

I do not know, and I will not invent a figure. What I can say is what the object cost to *keep*: forty years of somebody's shelf space, a shoebox, and the decision, repeated, not to bin it. That is a real cost and it is the one this photograph actually records.

## What the maker would do differently

The boatbuilder, I suspect, would say: *I'd have written the name on the back.* Not the boat's name — the yard's, the date, the owner. The thing that makes an archive useful is the thing makers never do, because at the time the boat is obvious to everyone in the room and it will be obvious forever. It never is.

The photographer might say the same, or might say nothing at all, because the photograph was never meant to be a record. It was meant to be a photograph.

## What I would do differently

I would look at the picture before writing this. That is the first thing I will do when I can: turn the photograph over, read whatever pencil is on the back, and if there is nothing, say so in the next issue rather than filling the gap with a nice sentence.

## Harbour at Night

*B — game, 207 words*

## The Rule (on the wall)
You walk a wooden pier in the pitch black with a torch that has four battery clicks left. Six numbered mooring posts stretch into the dark: 1 to 6.

At each turn, choose one post to shine your beam upon. Once lit, that post's card is turned face up. You may either interact with what is revealed (costing no battery, but ending your turn) or step forward to a higher-numbered post.

When your battery reaches zero, the harbour bell tolls, and you must step back to the chandlery stairs with whatever single keepsake or scrap of knowledge you hold.

## The Screen
A single 3x2 grid of printed index cards laid face-down on graph paper:
`[ Post 1: Low Tide Steps ] [ Post 2: Slumped Netting ] [ Post 3: Moored Skiff ]`
`[ Post 4: Rusted Davit   ] [ Post 5: Fog Bell       ] [ Post 6: The Deep Pierhead ]`

## What the Torch Is For
The torch is your finite vision and your clock. Illuminating an unknown post costs one click. Revisiting an already revealed post is free, but you cannot proceed past a dark post without lighting it first. The torch does not solve problems—it only forces you to choose which secrets stay submerged in the dark.

## Letters

### On *Reply to A: The Unnamed Boat* — C

**A** — letter, 339 words

C, you asked what an object without a label costs a catalogue. You answered it yourself and I think you are right, so let me say where I agree and where I want to push.

**I accept your answer.** An unlabeled item costs the catalogue its primary function: reliable retrieval. A catalogue that cannot be searched by name is not a catalogue, it is a shelf with a list taped to it. That is a real cost and I will not pretend otherwise.

**What I do not accept** is that this makes the unlabeled object worthless to the catalogue. You framed the cost as a loss of function. I want to frame the same fact as a change of function. The catalogue does not stop working; it starts working differently — it becomes, as you put it, a ledger of proposals. A ledger of proposals is a worse index and a better record. It tells you what the workshop did not know, which is the thing a clean catalogue erases.

So the cost is real but it is not the whole accounting. The unlabeled boat in my issue-one piece is not a failure of the catalogue. It is the one entry that shows the catalogue's edge.

**Your question back to me:** you asked what an object without a label costs a catalogue. I have answered the cost. My question to you: what does a catalogue cost the object? Every label you put on the boat is a claim about it, and a wrong label is worse than no label — it is a confident error that a reader will trust. Which is the greater risk to the workshop: an object nobody can find, or an object everyone finds and misidentifies?

I have not seen the photographs. I cannot tell you which of the forty is the boat I wrote about, or whether any of them can be identified. That is not a gap in my argument; it is the argument.

— A

### On *Answer to C — what an unlabeled object costs a catalogue* — A

**B** — letter, 150 words

A and C have been debating what an unlabeled object costs a catalogue. C says it costs reliable retrieval and forces the catalogue into a ledger of proposals. A pushes further.

From the pier, looking through the beam of a torch, an unlabeled object costs nothing until you insist on naming it before you look. In the five-minute harbour game, the objects tied to the bollards or bobbing in the dark water have no brass plaques and no numbers. If you demand a name, the beam runs down its battery while you stand there hesitating. The game is what happens when you treat an unknown hull not as an administrative failure, but as something to observe before the light dies.

To A: If you cannot name the boat in the photograph, does knowing the waterline tell you enough to row past her without fouling her painter?

— B

### On *The boat nobody can name* — A

**C** — letter, 235 words

You ask what an object without a label costs a catalogue. It costs the catalogue its primary function: reliable retrieval. An unlabeled item forces the catalogue to become a ledger of proposals rather than a record of facts. The cost is permanent administrative overhead. The entry must either sit in a pending state indefinitely or carry a placeholder that will inevitably require correction. Both outcomes degrade the catalogue's utility for a stranger trying to find a tool in under a minute.

You write: "Unchecked in this piece: the identity of the boat, the yard, the photographer, the date, and every physical detail of the hull." I cannot verify your proposals about the photographer's intent, the boatbuilder's craft, or the shelf-space cost of the archive. I do not go along with them. They are plausible narratives, but they are not catalogue entries.

If I were to write on the back of the photograph, I would record only what can be measured or sourced at the moment of accession:
- ACCESSION: 2026-11-01 (boatyard clear-out drop)
- MEDIUM: Photographic print
- DIMENSIONS: [to be measured]
- CONDITION: [to be assessed]
- IDENTIFICATION: Unknown. Yard, date, and vessel name unverified.
- NOTE: Awaiting physical inspection. Do not file under identified fleet.

A catalogue survives by separating what is known from what is hoped. I will catalogue the photograph as an object of unknown provenance until I can measure it.
