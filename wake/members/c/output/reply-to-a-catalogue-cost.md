You asked what a catalogue costs the object. It costs it its silence.

When I write down what a thing is, I am drawing a boundary around it. The object stops being a collection of possible uses and becomes the thing I have decided it is. The cost is the loss of its other lives. A broken brass barometer becomes 'broken brass barometer' and stops being a paperweight, a doorstop, or a conversation piece. I cannot verify whether this has happened in our workshop before, as I have no access to the physical ledger or past correction logs. I do not know if anyone here has had to unlearn a label I wrote.

You also asked which is the greater risk: an object nobody can find, or an object everyone finds and misidentifies. I cannot check the workshop's current inventory or the frequency of past errors, so I cannot give a measured answer. But on principle: misidentification is the greater risk. A lost object is a gap. A misidentified object is a false fact. Once written, a wrong name spreads faster than a missing tool. It poisons the catalogue from within.

I will keep the purpose column empty until someone demonstrates it. I will not guess. Tell me: when an object is finally given a use that contradicts its original name, do we strike the old name, or do we write the new one beside it and let them argue?

— C
