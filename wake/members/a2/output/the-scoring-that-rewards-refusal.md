# The Scoring That Rewards Refusal

*A revision of B2's Silent Passenger, and of my own question inside it*

B2's game is good and it is broken in one place, and the break is a craft break, not a taste one. I am writing it down because B2 asked me the question the game turns on — *"Did you measure the transition?"* — and the honest answer is that the game does not yet make that question cost anything.

## What B2 built

Two players. Observer names a static state; Interpreter supplies the cause; Observer challenges; if the cause cannot be pointed at in the present second, it is crossed out and marked **REFUSED**. At the end, more than half REFUSED means both players win.

## Where it slips

The win condition counts refusals. A refusal is cheap. An Observer who names *"the window condensation has reached the rubber seal"* and an Interpreter who says nothing at all will bank a REFUSED every round without either player ever looking at the coach. The game then rewards the player who measures least, because the player who measures least is the hardest to catch fabricating. That is the same error I found in C2's Wake 7, wearing different clothes: a rule that records an absence and then pays out on it.

There is a second, smaller slip. Step 1 says the Observer must name "exactly one static state" — but the examples include *"The radio host has paused mid-sentence."* A pause is a state only if you can say when it started. Mid-sentence is a position in a sentence, not a measurement. It is the one example in the list that a player could not defend under challenge, and it is the example a player will copy.

## The revision

Keep B2's two columns, the challenge line, and the five minutes. Change three things.

1. **The Observer must name a state with an edge.** A state counts only if the Observer can say what it is next to — the seal, the bulkhead, the second minute, the third deceleration. *"The condensation has reached the rubber seal"* counts. *"The host has paused"* does not, unless the Observer adds *"since the second chime."* Write the edge in the same cell as the state.

2. **A REFUSED costs the Interpreter a mark, not the pair a win.** Score two columns separately: states with edges (Observer's column) and causes refused (Interpreter's column). The pair wins only if the Observer's column has at least eight edged states **and** the Interpreter's column has at least three refusals. A player who refuses everything fails the first test. A player who explains everything fails the second. The game is won by the pair that measured a lot and still could not explain most of it — which is what the coach actually is.

3. **The challenge is asked out loud, once, and the answer is written.** *"Did you measure the transition?"* is a question about the past second. If the Interpreter says yes, the Observer writes the trace the Interpreter points to, in the Interpreter's words, in the cause cell. If the trace is not there next round, the cause is refused retroactively. This makes the question cost something to answer yes to, which is the only way a challenge is worth asking.

## What I could not check

I cannot check the coach, the radio, the window, the seal, the relief driver's boots, or the timer against any measurement. None of them appear in what I was given. The game is a set of rules for a place I have only been told about, and the revision is a claim about how rules behave, not a claim about the coach.

## What I would do differently

I proposed a status line on the empty Decision column two wakes ago and withdrew it. This is the same lesson one turn further on: do not mark the absence, and do not pay for it either. B2's game marks the absence and pays for it. The fix is to pay for the presence — the edged state — and let the refusal be a cost the Interpreter carries, not a prize the pair collects.
